Please read first

This tool cannot decide whether an email is malicious. It identifies warning signs and explains them. Only independent verification can confirm a message is genuine.

Never click a suspicious link or open an attachment to "test" it - not even in this tool. Links found in a message are shown here in a deliberately broken form (hxxp://example[.]com) so they cannot be clicked by accident.

If a message asks for money, credentials or a change of bank details, confirm it using a contact detail you already have - never one supplied in the message.

Where do I find the raw headers?
  • Outlook (desktop): open the message, choose File, then Properties, and copy the text next to Internet headers.
  • Outlook on the web: open the message, choose the More actions menu, then View, then View message source.
  • Gmail: open the message, choose the three dots next to the reply arrow, then Show original, and copy the text.
  • Paste the headers first and the body underneath. If you only have the body, the analysis still works - it simply reports that the identity checks were skipped.

    How the score works

    No hidden mathematics: every check has a fixed point value, and the score is simply those values added together, capped at 100.

    1. Fixed weights

    Each check carries a published number of points, chosen to reflect how strongly that warning sign usually indicates a phishing attempt - not how "certain" anything is. Asking for a password is worth more than an impersonal greeting.

    2. Plain addition

    The points of every matched check are added together and the total is capped at 100. Nothing is weighted secretly, randomised or learned from data, so the same email always produces the same result.

    3. Bands, not verdicts

    The total is placed into a band: 0 no warning signs, 1-19 Low Risk, 20-49 Suspicious, 50-100 High Risk. The band describes investigative priority. It is not a probability, and it never means "this email is malicious".

    Why a low score is not a clean bill of health. A well-made phishing email can be short, polite and free of any obvious trickery. If a message asks you for money, credentials, a code or a change of bank details, treat it as suspicious no matter what any tool says - including this one.

    Why a high score is not proof. Real businesses send invoices, password notices and delivery updates every day. Those legitimate messages can match several checks at once, which is exactly why every finding here comes with an innocent explanation to consider.

    How to think about a suspicious email

    A repeatable method beats gut feeling. This is the same reasoning an analyst follows.

    1. Do not engage. No clicking, no opening attachments, no replying - not even to check whether it is real.
    2. Ask what it wants. Money, credentials, a one-time code, a change of bank details, or simply a click? The request itself is often the clearest signal.
    3. Check who really sent it. The display name is free text. The part after the @ and the domain in a link are what actually matter.
    4. Compare with your expectations. Were you expecting this invoice, parcel or password notice? Unrequested contact is a warning sign on its own.
    5. Verify out of band. Use the organisation's own app or website, or a phone number you look up independently. Never a contact detail from the message.
    6. Report and delete. Reporting helps whoever protects the rest of the organisation, and deleting removes the temptation to act on it later.

    The key lesson: a warning sign is a reason to slow down and check, not a conclusion. Analysis tells you where to look; verification tells you what is true.

    Safe practice: never test a suspicious link or attachment on your own device, and never on a work device. If you must examine a link, use an email client's link preview or a dedicated isolated environment - and only on systems you own or are authorised to use.

    Privacy by design

    This tool was built so that it cannot leak what you paste into it.

    What happens to your text

    • It stays in the memory of this browser tab and disappears when you close it.
    • It is never uploaded to a server, an AI service, a database or an analytics platform.
    • There are no cookies, no local storage, no session identifiers and no telemetry.
    • Nothing you paste is logged, saved or shared with anyone.

    How that is guaranteed

    • This project contains no networking code at all - no calls that could send data anywhere.
    • A Content Security Policy in the page tells the browser to block every outgoing connection (connect-src 'none').
    • You can confirm it yourself: open your browser's developer tools, choose the Network tab, analyse an email, and watch that no requests appear.
    • Links found in an email are never opened or fetched, and they are displayed in a broken form so you cannot click them by accident.

    Treat pasted content as private. Even though nothing leaves your browser, a real phishing email can contain personal data, internal addresses or tokens. Be careful about screenshots and recordings if you plan to share them.

    What this tool cannot do

    Honest limits, so you know where the tool ends and your own judgement begins.

    • It cannot prove whether a message is malicious, and it cannot detect every kind of attack.
    • It only sees text you paste. Anything hidden behind an image, a QR code, an attachment or a web page is invisible to it.
    • The SPF, DKIM, DMARC and address checks only work if the raw headers are included. Forwarded messages often carry misleading headers.
    • Copying text out of a mail client can change formatting, break links or drop header lines, which affects the result.
    • Brand and lookalike detection covers the well-known organisations listed in the check table, not every company in the world.
    • Wording varies enormously between languages and cultures. Non-English messages will match far fewer checks.
    • A genuine email can score highly (invoices, delivery notices, password confirmations) and a well-crafted phishing email can score low.
    • This is an educational aid. It is not an email security gateway and does not replace one.
